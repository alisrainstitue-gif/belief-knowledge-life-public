When Everything Became a Question Mark · Chapter One · Entry 04

Where Did the Science End?

2 October 2026·English

The lecture had frightened me because it made sense.

That was what made it difficult to shake off.

For a day or two, I carried its conclusions with me almost as one solid thing: evolution, complexity, design, Allah Subhanahu wa Taala, the brain, the mind. They had come to me in one smooth sequence, and in my head they were still sitting together.

Eventually, I started going back over it more slowly.

Not looking for a way out. Just trying to understand what exactly I had heard.

The first part was easy to recognise.

Evolution. Natural selection. Biological complexity.

I understood the basic argument: complex structures do not have to appear all at once. Small changes can accumulate over generations. Natural selection can preserve variations that help survival and reproduction. Something that looks designed does not necessarily require each part to have been individually designed.

So far, I was still on familiar ground.

Then I reached the watch.

The argument was simple. If you found a watch, its complexity would make you think there had been a watchmaker. So when we look at life, which is vastly more complex, should we infer a designer?

The lecture then gave reasons not to.

Natural selection could account for complexity.

Some biological structures did not look especially well designed.

And if complexity itself required a Creator, then the obvious question followed: who created the Creator?

I had heard all of this without any difficulty.

But now, going through it again, I found myself stopping at one point.

Natural selection could explain how biological complexity develops.

Yes.

But how had I moved from there to the existence of a Creator?

I went back again.

The biology had explained a mechanism.

The watchmaker discussion was asking what that mechanism meant for an argument about Allah Subhanahu wa Taala.

Those two things were connected. But suddenly they did not look like exactly the same kind of claim.

That was the first crack in what had felt, until then, like one solid conclusion.

Then I thought about the brain.

The lecture had described mental life through the physical brain. That had felt equally straightforward while I was listening.

But when I slowed that down too, I noticed another small gap.

Saying that changes in the brain affect thought, emotion and behaviour made complete sense to me.

Saying that mental life is therefore entirely physical seemed to be saying something more.

I could feel the difference, but I could not explain it.

And if I could not explain it, how did I know I was not simply protecting what I wanted to believe?
