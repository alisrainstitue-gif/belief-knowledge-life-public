When Everything Became a Question Mark · Chapter One · Entry 01

I Thought I Had Found My Place

English

For a while, I found myself enrolling in one course after another.

There was always something more to learn, but what drew me in was how one question kept opening into another. A surah I had read many times could suddenly look different because of one word in Arabic. A discussion about human behaviour could take me back to something I had studied in science. Different areas of learning kept meeting each other in unexpected ways.

I loved that.

By then, conventional career ambition had already lost much of its hold on me. Science had not. I still loved research, data, questions, patterns, and that particular satisfaction of finding a connection that had not been obvious before. I simply no longer felt that titles, senior positions or professional success were enough to organise a life around.

Part of that change came from how I had begun to think about success itself.

If I believed that this life was only one part of a much longer existence, then building everything around what could be achieved in sixty or seventy years began to feel strangely limited. I still wanted to grow, learn and do something worthwhile. But I wanted that growth to matter beyond the boundaries of this life. Success had to mean more than how far I could go professionally before my time here ended.

That was one reason learning Deen mattered so much to me.

It was intellectually engaging, but it was also connected to what I understood as the purpose of my existence. The questions were not only about what I could know, but about what kind of life was worth building if this life was not the whole story.

Then another possibility slowly began to take shape.

Perhaps the abilities I already had and the world of Deen I was entering did not have to remain separate.

The people teaching Deen had spent years learning things I had not: Arabic, Qur'an, tafsir and formal religious scholarship. I brought a different set of skills: research, technology, systems, policy, organisation and communication.

I began wondering what might become possible if those strengths could work together.

Perhaps I could help build the infrastructure around their work, helping their knowledge reach more people while also helping them navigate the increasingly technology-based systems through which modern learning and education are organised and delivered.

Perhaps the skills I had spent years developing could serve something that felt more meaningful to me than career progression alone.

And perhaps, in doing that, I had found something I had been looking for without quite knowing how to name it.

My passion for science, learning and building things did not have to sit separately from what I believed my life was ultimately for.

They could meet in the same work.

For the first time in a long while, the pieces of the puzzle seemed to be coming together.

My passion and my purpose were no longer pulling in different directions.

I thought I had found a place where they could belong together.
