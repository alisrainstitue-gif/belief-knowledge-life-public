Behind the way we act

  • Reading the Qur’an
  • Belief and conduct
  • Science and interpretation

I have come back to the request in Fatiha: “Guide us to the straight path.” By this point, I have already acknowledged Allah Subhanahu wa Taala, His mercy, the Day of Judgment, whom I worship and where I seek help. Those commitments are already there. What am I asking to understand and live by when I ask for guidance?

I keep thinking about the orientation behind an action. What does a person believe is happening? What are they trying to achieve? Which assumptions make a particular response seem right to them? The action is what I can see. Following those questions takes me into the thinking that produced it.

This matters because situations change. If I read an account of someone’s conduct and retain only the final act, I may recognise that act when it appears again. Understanding the reasons and principles involved could help me recognise a similar direction in a very different situation. There are firm commitments and clear actions in Deen. I am trying to understand how they enter a person’s judgment as life unfolds.

Alongside these thoughts, I am reading Judith Rich Harris’s The Nurture Assumption. I have not finished it, and I am still working out what to make of her argument. She questions the weight given to parents in explanations of how children develop. In her account, Freud and the behaviourists arrived at very different theories while both giving early experiences with parents a central place. She brings behavioural-genetic research into that discussion and asks us to look more closely at the wider environment.

The part I keep returning to is the child among other children. Harris calls her proposal “group socialization theory”. Peers, belonging and the patterns of a group have a major role in her explanation of how children learn to behave outside the home. I initially reached for “anti-socialisation” while trying to describe the book. “Challenging parent-centred accounts of socialisation” captures the argument more accurately.

That widens the picture I am considering. Parents are involved in a child’s life in several ways, including through the places they live, the schools they choose and the people around them. Genes, relationships and opportunities also belong in the picture. I want to understand how these influences interact. How much each explains is something I still need to examine for the particular outcome being studied.

This reading has brought the communal language of Fatiha back into focus for me. We worship. We seek help. Guide us. Then the path is described in relation to people. The request gives me a place among others, and I find myself thinking about how people learn a way of living together: what becomes familiar, what earns approval, what a group comes to take for granted.

I want to bring that attention to the Qur’an’s accounts of individuals and communities. Alongside what people do, I want to follow the beliefs, reasons and intentions the text gives us, and the social setting in which their choices unfold. What made a response persuasive to them? What did they value? Which patterns might I recognise in another setting, including my own?

I also needed more precise names for these questions. Social psychology is especially relevant to how we understand other people and respond to groups. Cognitive psychology helps with beliefs, interpretation and decisions. For the childhood questions, developmental psychology and behavioural genetics address different parts of the picture. Sociology widens the view to institutions and culture, while philosophy of action asks about reasons, purposes and agency. These give me places to look and more careful questions to ask as I read.

When I tried to explain the connection, I moved between “psychology”, “mind” and “worldview”. Then I reached for “intention”. That felt closer to an important part of what I meant: the purpose and understanding from which an action comes. I want to understand the relationship between those things, especially when the beliefs a person affirms and the life they are learning to live pull in different directions.

For now, this is where the question of guidance has taken me. Having affirmed those beliefs at the beginning of Fatiha, I am wondering how they become the orientation with which I meet the next situation, among the people with whom I live.

Reading alongside this entry

  • Al-Fatiha 1:1–7. The plural forms in 1:5–7 are the textual starting point; the connection explored here is my developing reflection.
  • Judith Rich Harris, The Nurture Assumption, publisher’s excerpt, and her group socialization theory paper. Harris’s explanation is a theory to examine alongside other evidence.
  • American Psychological Association: social psychology, cognitive psychology and developmental psychology.
