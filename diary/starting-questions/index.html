Finding something worth doing

I need to engage in deep thought. That has been a real need, not something I can put aside by keeping myself occupied. I want to understand things, and I want the effort of understanding to become part of something worthwhile. Ordinary stability matters, but it does not answer the question of what I should do with the abilities I have.

Science is still very much part of this. I enjoy following a question, reading what has been found, comparing explanations and trying to work out what the evidence allows us to say. That interest does not disappear when a conventional professional path no longer provides the meaning I had hoped for. I can question the direction of my work without losing my interest in the work of understanding.

Learning Deen brought that intellectual excitement into another part of my life. There was so much to learn, and there seemed to be a way of doing something useful with what I already knew. I hoped to work with people who had spent years studying religious sources. They could contribute that knowledge; I could contribute practical and technical skills. The possibility of bringing those things together felt meaningful.

What made it worthwhile was my understanding of life beyond these sixty or so years. Success meant something that could matter beyond this life. In that work, my passion for science and learning and my sense of purpose in Deen seemed to come together. I was intellectually engaged, learning new things and doing something I believed was worth doing.

What I expected from teachers

Working more closely with religious teachers brought disappointment. There was a gap between the values I expected that learning to cultivate and the conduct I experienced. I do not want to turn this into an account of particular people. What has stayed with me is the question it raised: how can someone spend so much time studying and teaching something, yet struggle to live it?

I had wanted the practical work to have clear responsibilities, rights and obligations. A commitment affects other members of a team; it cannot be treated as if it concerns only the person making it. That was the point of accountability for me. We were trying to do something together, and our own preferences and egos could not come ahead of the work and everyone else involved.

I do not think these were malicious people. They were ordinary human beings, like you and me. But recognising that did not remove the disturbance. I had expected a relationship between religious knowledge and character. If that relationship is less straightforward than I assumed, I need to understand what is missing between knowing, believing and acting.

I had also encountered integrity in people who did not share my religious beliefs. That mattered because it complicated the picture. I could not explain character simply by asking which beliefs someone professed or how much religious knowledge they possessed. Nor could disappointment with a teacher tell me whether the religion was true. These were different questions, even though the experience had brought them together for me.

And then another thought: what if the difficulty is not simply that a person says one thing and does another? What if the way they understand the teaching already makes room for the way they act? How would I know? I began wondering how their worldview entered the interpretation itself, not only whether their behaviour matched what they taught.

That is particularly difficult when I need the teacher to reach the source. In English, I can read different opinions and follow the arguments myself. My Arabic was still basic. Perhaps answers existed that I could not yet reach. So I needed help, but I also needed to understand what I was receiving through that help. How could I judge an interpretation without having enough access to what was being interpreted?

The psychology lecture

Around the same period, I began studying psychology. It connected with questions I already had about human behaviour, with my scientific interests and with my study of the Qur’an. I wanted to understand human beings more deeply. I was approaching it as a learner, not looking for a dispute between scientific knowledge and faith.

During a lecture about the brain, evolution and behaviour, the explanation was coherent. Everything flowed logically. This was a knowledgeable lecturer speaking in his field, and I followed the account as science.

Afterwards, I was frightened. For a day or two, it felt as though science was proving something against my beliefs. I was already disappointed with my Deen teachers, and now the science seemed to be taking away something much deeper. My beliefs felt shaken, as though I might lose them. I did not immediately turn this into an argument I knew how to answer.

Only later, when I slowed down and thought it through, did I recognise the movement into philosophy. My scientific training helped me untangle it. There were findings, explanations of those findings, and claims about what a human being ultimately is. I needed to examine how one led to the next. What had the evidence established, and what further reasoning had been added?

The distinction I later put into words was quite specific. Natural selection explaining how biological complexity develops was one thing; what that explanation meant for an argument about a Creator was another. Similarly, changes in the brain affecting thought, emotion and behaviour made sense to me. The conclusion that mental life was therefore entirely physical seemed to say something more. But could I explain the difference, or was I only protecting what I wanted to believe?

One question from the lecture stayed with me. If a complex design requires a creator, would that reasoning also require a creator for God? And if God does not require one, why must something else that is complex require one? I needed to understand what this argument did and did not establish.

The same learner in both places

I do not differentiate between learning psychology and learning tafsir, Qur’anic interpretation, in the way people sometimes expect. The subjects are different, but I am still the same person trying to understand. I ask questions, look at the relevant sources, consider alternatives and check whether a conclusion follows. That is why I can question a psychology professor and a tafsir teacher without feeling that I have changed sides.

In a religious class, a teacher spoke about human beings before Adam. That brought me to questions about evolution and how Muslim teachers were trying to connect it with the Qur’an. I was not agreeing or disagreeing with evolution. My discomfort was with the method: were we examining an interpretation, or trying to make it fit something that now carried scientific authority?

If an interpretation has changed, I want to understand why. What does the text support? What did earlier interpreters understand? What new evidence or argument makes another reading reasonable? I do not want a newer reconciliation presented as though it had always been the obvious meaning, but I also cannot assume that every new reading is merely giving in to pressure. That requires study.

I began watching material about genetics and human origins, trying to understand how the accounts connected. There were other kinds of humans to learn about, and questions about what genetics could tell us. I had only begun. Could our religious understanding be mapped onto what was being described? Would that be justified, or would I be forcing the pieces together?

Then there was the question of the first cell. My thoughts moved between the development of living things, the beginning of life and the existence of a Creator. If a process could be explained, would that settle why it existed? Could creation happen through a process, rather than mean something appearing without one? These were questions I wanted to pursue. I had not worked through the science, and I could not treat my objections as answers.

Being among people and still feeling alone

A long social gathering made the loneliness unusually clear. I was surrounded by people, but I had almost nothing I wanted to contribute to the ordinary conversation. My mind was occupied with these questions about evidence, belief, interpretation and trust. When I tried to bring them into conversation, it tended to become a one-way explanation rather than something we could explore together.

I do not mean that other people were shallow, or that their interests were unworthy. The problem was that the things occupying me did not seem to have a place. I wanted someone to stay with a difficult question, bring a different argument, challenge a premise, or show me evidence I had missed. I did not need everyone to agree with me. I wanted a conversation that could move.

“I don’t fit anywhere.” That was how I expressed it. Scientific questions, religious commitment and the difficulties of ordinary life were all present together for me, yet I struggled to find a place where they could remain in the same conversation. It felt as though everything around me had become a question mark.

When I said I felt lost, I was not saying that my connection with Allah Subhanahu wa Taala had disappeared. My thirst for understanding was not being met. I could not find where this need belonged, where I could ask these questions while remaining myself.

I was not looking for someone whose confidence I could borrow indefinitely. I wanted intellectual companionship, including the possibility of being shown that I was wrong. “Where are they right? Where am I wrong?” That question expresses the position more accurately than an argument for either side.

Why philosophy became necessary

Philosophy entered because these questions were already there. I had not set out with a plan to become absorbed in a new subject. I needed language for the problems I was encountering: what counts as knowledge, what science can establish, what a human being is, and how an explanation comes to seem complete.

The question about teachers also belonged here. When I learn from an interpretation, how do I distinguish the source from what the interpreter has contributed? And when I listen to a scientific explanation, where does the model end and a wider understanding of reality enter? I could not settle either question simply by deciding which kind of authority I preferred.

But I was part of this encounter too. I could not assume that everyone else was wrong and I alone was seeing clearly. Which of my own assumptions or understandings had contributed to the disappointment? What was in the source, what had the person explaining it added, and what was I bringing to it myself?

I was also thinking about the life around us. Achievement, consumption, productivity and entertainment are immediate. Their rewards are visible. Belief in the unseen and the Hereafter can be harder to connect with daily priorities, especially when Deen is communicated mainly as obligations to perform and consequences to come. I felt the difficulty of that connection myself.

Could we consciously affirm one understanding of life while gradually organising our attention around another? Could the things we repeatedly pursue change what feels important, even while our stated beliefs remain the same? I do not yet know how far that explains anything. But it gives me a question about what is shaping us while we think we are simply studying, working and getting on with life.

So the subjects I need may include psychology, philosophy, sociology, genetics, Qur’an and Arabic. I do not want a list of disciplines to become another plan that takes the place of learning. I want the question to lead me to the work it needs. A source should help me test or develop the thought, including showing that I have misunderstood, rather than replace it with a ready-made answer.

I want to record this while it is happening. The confusion matters, but so do the excitement of learning and the moments when a question becomes clearer. If I share only conclusions, I lose the part I most want to preserve: how I came to ask, what I thought the answer might be, and what made me reconsider it.

Perhaps one person will need this someday. Maybe someone will feel this same difficulty and find a question here that they recognise. I also want serious academic work to emerge where there is something worth developing, something another person could find and use in a literature review. But I do not know yet what that work will be. I want to record the problems as well as the conclusions.
